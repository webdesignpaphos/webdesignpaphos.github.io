Colour is one of the first things a visitor processes when they land on your website. Research consistently shows that visual impressions form within 50 milliseconds, and colour accounts for a significant share of that snap judgement. Before anyone reads your headline, your palette has already signalled something about your brand: whether you are trustworthy, modern, affordable, premium, creative, or clinical.

Yet many business websites are built with whatever colours the owner happened to like at the time, or whatever the designer pulled from the logo without a structured system behind it. The result is often a site that looks inconsistent, feels hard to read, or simply does not convert as well as it should.

This guide covers the practical process of choosing a colour palette for a business website: how to structure it, which tools to use, how to check accessibility, and how to test your choices before committing to a full build.

## Why Your Colour Palette Is a Business Decision, Not Just a Style Choice

Colour influences behaviour, not just perception. Studies from brand consistency research have found that consistent use of colour can increase brand recognition by as much as 80 percent. On a website, that recognition effect compounds: a visitor who has seen your social posts, your business card, or your van signage arrives at your site and instantly connects the palette to your brand, reinforcing trust.

Conversion is the other dimension. Your call-to-action buttons, your contact form, your pricing section: all of these depend on colour contrast to draw attention in the right order. A poorly chosen accent colour can make your primary button invisible against a busy background. A neutral that is slightly too dark can make your body text exhausting to read, pushing visitors away before they get to the information they need.

For businesses in Cyprus competing for online visibility, a professional and coherent colour palette is one of the fastest ways to close the gap between a DIY site and a site that earns genuine trust.

## The Anatomy of a Website Colour Palette

A well-structured website palette has four components, not just two or three:

**Primary colour.** This is your dominant brand colour, the one most strongly associated with your identity. It often comes from your logo, though it does not have to. The primary colour sets the tone for the entire site.

**Secondary colour.** This supports the primary and adds depth. It is used in section backgrounds, card borders, icon fills, and other structural elements. The relationship between primary and secondary defines the character of the design: a complementary pairing creates contrast and energy, an analogous pairing creates harmony and calm.

**Accent colour.** This is a deliberate contrast point. It should appear sparingly and it should always be used for the actions you most want visitors to take: primary buttons, key links, badges, and highlights. A good accent colour grabs attention precisely because the rest of the palette does not use it.

**Neutrals.** These are the unsung heroes: off-whites, light greys, mid greys, dark charcoals, and near-blacks. Neutrals carry the majority of your body text, section dividers, card backgrounds, and headings. Picking the wrong neutral is one of the most common reasons a site feels "off" without anyone being able to explain why. A warm off-white pairs naturally with warm brand colours; a cool blue-white suits a corporate palette built on cool blues and greens.

## The 60-30-10 Rule

This is the most practical structural rule in web design colour work, and it applies at the page level. The idea is simple: 60 percent of any page view should be dominated by your primary or neutral colour, 30 percent by your secondary, and 10 percent by your accent.

In practice this means:

- Your background and large sections (hero background, main content areas) use the dominant 60 percent colour, often a light neutral or your primary colour in a very muted form.
- Your secondary fills structural elements: the header, the footer, card containers, sidebar columns, section bands.
- Your accent appears only on buttons, badges, and critical highlights.

The effect of this structure is that your accent always stands out because it is outnumbered. When the accent colour appears everywhere, it stops being special. When it appears only 10 percent of the time, visitors instinctively notice it. This is the visual mechanism behind why a well-placed CTA button converts better than a poorly placed one, even when the copy is identical.

Real examples of this rule in practice include sites like Mailchimp (white and neutral photography as the dominant tone, natural warm accents as secondary, yellow as the standout accent), and Hipcamp (light grey backgrounds with green accents for its nature-focused brand). The proportions vary slightly, but the underlying logic is the same.

## Best Tools for Building Your Palette

There are several strong tools for this work, and it is worth knowing what each is best at.

### Coolors (coolors.co)

Coolors is the most widely used palette generator among designers. You start with a single seed colour and it generates complementary shades, which you can lock or shuffle until the palette feels right. It also lets you extract a palette from an uploaded image, which is useful if you are starting from a brand photograph or a piece of physical collateral. The built-in contrast checker shows WCAG scores for each colour pair, which saves you a separate step.

### Adobe Color (color.adobe.com)

Adobe Color works from a colour wheel. You choose a harmony rule (complementary, analogous, split-complementary, triadic, square) and drag one node on the wheel to generate the others. This tool is better when you understand colour theory and want to work from a principled starting point rather than exploration. It integrates with Creative Cloud, so if your designer uses Photoshop or Illustrator, palettes built here are easy to share.

### Paletton (paletton.com)

Paletton is older but still excellent for exploring tonal ranges. It shows not just the five base swatches but all their lighter and darker variants, which is important when you need to choose how light or dark a section background should be.

### WebAIM Contrast Checker (webaim.org/resources/contrastchecker)

This is not a palette generator but a checking tool, and it is essential. You enter a foreground colour and a background colour and it tells you the contrast ratio and whether it passes WCAG AA or AAA thresholds. Run every text-on-background combination in your palette through this before finalising.

### Khroma (khroma.co)

Khroma uses a trained model to generate palettes based on the colours you rate. If you find other generators too algorithmic and want something that feels more curated, Khroma is worth exploring. It learns from your preferences and surfaces combinations that align with your taste.

## Accessibility and Contrast Ratios: Numbers That Matter

The WebAIM Million 2026 report found that 83.9 percent of the top one million homepages have at least one low-contrast text failure. That means the vast majority of websites are failing a basic accessibility standard that is also a legal requirement in many countries.

The WCAG 2.2 Level AA standards specify:

- **4.5:1** minimum contrast ratio between normal body text and its background.
- **3:1** minimum for large text (18px regular or 14px bold and above).
- **3:1** minimum for interactive components and graphical elements such as icons.

Level AAA sets a higher bar of **7:1** for normal text and **4.5:1** for large text.

These ratios are not arbitrary. They are calibrated against the range of visual impairments common in the general population. A site that fails 4.5:1 on body text is effectively unreadable for a significant percentage of your potential customers.

To check your palette, plug each text-and-background pair into WebAIM's checker. Check:

- Body text on your main background.
- Headings on your section backgrounds.
- Button label text on your accent colour.
- Link text in-body.
- Any text placed over photography or gradients.

That last case (text on images) is the one most often overlooked. A semi-transparent dark overlay behind text on a photograph is not decoration: it is a contrast fix.

If a colour pair fails, do not abandon the colour. Adjust the lightness value: darken the text or lighten the background until the ratio passes. Coolors' built-in contrast view and the Colour Contrast Analyser desktop app from TPGi both let you make these micro-adjustments while watching the ratio update in real time.

## Colour and Industry Expectations

Different industries carry different colour associations in the minds of consumers, and violating those expectations requires strong creative justification. A few broad patterns hold across markets:

**Finance and professional services:** Deep blues, dark greys, and whites. These signal stability, precision, and trust. A financial firm using bright orange as its dominant colour faces a higher barrier to establishing credibility.

**Health and wellness:** Greens (nature, health, life) and blues (calm, clinical trust) dominate. Bright reds are generally avoided except for urgency or pharmaceutical contexts.

**Hospitality, food, and tourism:** Warmer palettes work well. Earthy tones and terracotta suit traditional or local restaurant brands; fresh greens and whites suit health-focused cafes; navy and gold suggest premium experiences.

**Construction and trades:** Bold, high-contrast palettes are common: dark navy, construction orange or yellow, white. They signal strength and visibility.

For a business based in Paphos or anywhere in Cyprus, it is worth considering how your palette reads on both a desktop screen in an office and on a phone screen in bright Mediterranean sunlight. High contrast helps legibility in bright outdoor conditions, which is a practical argument for meeting WCAG thresholds beyond compliance.

## Testing Your Palette Before You Commit

Choosing colours on a design tool and seeing them deployed across a real website are very different experiences. Before you finalise a palette:

**Preview it on multiple devices.** The same palette can look warmer or cooler depending on screen calibration. An iPhone screen renders colour differently from a Windows laptop.

**Check it in direct light.** Take a phone outside and view your design mock-up in sunlight. Does the text remain readable? Do the accent colours still stand out?

**Run a colour blindness simulation.** Around 8 percent of men and 0.5 percent of women have some form of colour vision deficiency. Tools like Coblis (coblis.colblindor.com) simulate how your palette looks to someone with deuteranopia, protanopia, or tritanopia. If your primary and accent colours blend into each other under any simulation, you need to add shape or pattern differentiation, or adjust one of the colours.

**Show it to someone outside your industry.** Your accountant, your colleague from a different sector, your partner. Ask them what the palette makes them think of. If they say "medical clinic" and you run a boutique hotel, something needs to change.

**Build a simple style tile.** A style tile is a one-page document showing your palette swatches, a headline in your chosen font, body text, a primary button, and a secondary button. It takes 20 minutes to make and gives you a test canvas that is far more informative than five colour swatches alone.

## Common Colour Mistakes to Avoid

**Using too many colours.** More than five colours in a palette (including neutrals) is usually too many for a service business website. Each additional colour increases the cognitive complexity for visitors and makes the design harder to maintain consistently as the site grows.

**Ignoring neutral selection.** Choosing a pure white (#ffffff) and pure black (#000000) as your neutrals is a common shortcut that produces harsh, clinical results. An off-white like #f8f5f1 and a near-black like #1c1c1e are warmer and easier to read for extended periods.

**Using your logo colour for everything.** A logo is designed to work at small sizes on many backgrounds. A web palette needs to perform across large areas, small text, and interactive elements. Your logo's primary colour may not be appropriate for button backgrounds or large hero sections.

**Not testing contrast early.** Designers sometimes check contrast at the end, after a full layout has been built. Retrofitting contrast fixes at that stage means adjusting colours that are already baked into dozens of components. Test contrast at the palette stage, before a line of layout code is written.

**Copying a competitor directly.** Benchmark what your competitors use, but do not replicate it. Your palette needs to signal both industry credibility (meeting category expectations) and brand distinctiveness (giving customers a reason to remember you specifically).
